# Non-Asymmetric Risk Review Rubric

Read this reference whenever performing a full review. It supplies decision anchors, not a mechanical scorecard.

## Status Vocabulary

Use one status per dimension:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Symmetric | Meaningful upside and downside are borne by actors with decision power; harmed parties have protection or recourse. |
| Partly symmetric | Some exposure or accountability exists, but material downside remains externalized or avoidable. |
| Asymmetric | Decision-makers or recommenders capture upside while operators, users, the public, or future actors absorb material downside. |
| Unknown | The submitted text does not support a responsible judgment. Name the missing evidence. |
| Not applicable | The dimension genuinely does not affect this decision; explain briefly. |

Do not average these statuses. Severity depends on downside magnitude, reversibility, concentration, consent, and ability to exit.

## 1. Skin Check — Benefits and Costs

Determine:

- Who receives money, promotion, credit, influence, learning, or optionality if the proposal succeeds?
- Who pays implementation, incident, rework, delay, compliance, opportunity, and reputational costs if it fails?
- Can the recommender leave before delayed losses arrive?
- Are implementers or affected parties able to refuse, exit, appeal, or obtain compensation?
- Does the system create “success belongs to the proposer; failure belongs to the team” incentives?

Strong symmetry usually requires preassigned ownership of implementation and incidents, exposure proportional to decision power, recourse for involuntary risk-bearers, and no easy escape from delayed consequences.

## 2. Portfolio Check — Real Operating Exposure

Determine whether the proposal is:

- a concept, demo, slogan, or isolated success;
- a pilot under realistic constraints;
- repeated operating practice with observed failures and full costs;
- a recommendation from someone who executed it, funded it, maintained it, or personally absorbed comparable downside.

Ask for denominators, base rates, total cost of ownership, failed attempts, maintenance burden, exception handling, and evidence across relevant conditions. Direct experience strengthens evidence but is not mandatory: a proposer may rely on credible operators and data if accountability and exposure remain real.

## 3. Accountability Check — Reputation Stake and Exit

Convert vague “Karma” into an auditable mechanism:

- Write the recommendation, assumptions, predicted outcome, scope, metric, and deadline into a decision log before execution.
- Name the recommender, approver, implementer, metric owner, incident owner, and reviewer.
- Predeclare review dates, stop thresholds, remediation duties, and proportionate consequences for systematic miscalibration or concealed conflicts.
- Preserve predictions and failures; do not allow quiet deletion, metric substitution, or selective credit.
- Update trust using repeated calibrated outcomes and relevant new evidence. Do not call a process Bayesian unless priors, evidence, update rule, and decision threshold are inspectable.
- Provide an appeal path and distinguish bad luck from negligent process, deception, or repeated overconfidence.

Reputation penalties alone are weak when actors can rebrand, transfer, leave, or impose much larger losses on others.

## 4. Ergodicity Check — Ruin and Absorbing States

Test the path, not only the average outcome. Look for:

- bankruptcy, injury, fatality, permanent rights loss, unrecoverable data loss, regulatory exclusion, systemic cascade, monopoly lock-in, trust collapse, or destruction of future options;
- correlated failures and repeated bets that make individually small risks accumulate;
- hidden concentration, leverage, dependency, single points of failure, and irreversible migrations;
- rollback plans that exist on paper but are too slow, costly, or unauthorized to use.

Ask whether exposure is capped, the blast radius is isolated, the pilot is representative, rollback is rehearsed, a kill switch has an empowered owner, and the system can survive repeated failures. Fire the ruin override when a plausible severe absorbing state is uncapped or imposed on non-consenting parties, even if expected benefits look positive.

## 5. Courage Check — Real Public Stake

Determine:

- What concrete professional, financial, legal, social, or operational cost does the speaker risk by making this recommendation?
- Is the claim specific, time-bounded, falsifiable, and preserved for later review?
- Does the speaker accept the same rule when it harms their own interests or allies?
- Is “public good,” “safety,” “innovation,” or similar language connected to evidence, constraints, and personally accepted duties?
- Does the proposal protect a principle consistently, or selectively serve a special request while creating plausible deniability?

Do not infer hypocrisy or self-interest from tone. Treat moral packaging without operational commitments as weak evidence, then specify what would verify or refute the concern.

## Invalid Noise Rules

A statement may be excluded from the decision when one or more defects are material:

- no observable claim or decision implication;
- no accountable owner, scope, cost, constraint, or time horizon;
- recommendation cannot be falsified because success criteria can move after the fact;
- decisive downside is assigned to parties without control, consent, recourse, or exit;
- the speaker claims upside or moral credit while disclaiming implementation and failure ownership;
- an anecdote, credential, demo, or slogan is presented as sufficient operating evidence;
- moral urgency is used to suppress basic questions about evidence, rollback, or affected parties.

Classify only the defective statement as noise when the rest of the proposal remains testable. “Insufficient evidence” is usually more accurate than “noise” for a plausible but unverified proposal.

## Final Verdict Rules

| Verdict | Use when |
|---|---|
| Accept | Material risks are bounded and reversible, exposure is reasonably symmetric, and critical claims have adequate evidence. |
| Accept with symmetry conditions | The idea is testable, but named ownership, exposure caps, rollback, metrics, or accountability must be added before commitment. |
| Reject as asymmetrically risky | Material downside is externalized, non-consensual, or practically unbounded, especially when the ruin override fires. |
| Insufficient evidence | Critical facts or actor exposures are unknown and different answers would reverse the decision. |
| Invalid noise | The recommendation lacks stable, falsifiable decision content even after extracting its strongest charitable interpretation. |

Confidence reflects evidence quality, not rhetorical certainty:

- **High:** the text and supplied records directly establish the incentive and risk structure.
- **Moderate:** major relationships are supported but some consequential dependencies remain unverified.
- **Low:** the verdict rests heavily on inferences or missing operational evidence.
- **Unknown:** the available text cannot support a directional conclusion.

## Human Fact-Check Plan

Prioritize checks that could reverse the verdict or reveal ruin. For each check, record:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Claim or question | Exact factual assertion, incentive hypothesis, or dependency to test. |
| Evidence needed | Contract, budget, incident log, source data, experiment record, approval trail, staffing plan, insurance, rollback rehearsal, conflict disclosure, or comparable artifact. |
| Source or custodian | Person or system that controls the original record; prefer independent or primary sources. |
| Method | Inspect originals, reconcile totals and denominators, interview implementers and affected parties separately, reproduce a test, or compare predeclared predictions with outcomes. |
| Disconfirming result | Observation that would weaken or reverse the preliminary finding. |
| Priority | P0 for possible ruin or irreversible harm; P1 for verdict-changing exposure; P2 for useful calibration. |

At minimum, ask the human reviewer to:

1. Verify the proposer's actual authority, incentives, compensation, conflicts, implementation duties, and ability to exit.
2. Interview at least one operator and one materially affected party outside the proposer’s reporting line when feasible.
3. Inspect complete outcome data, including failures, denominators, maintenance costs, and selection criteria—not only showcased successes.
4. Confirm who legally and operationally owns incidents, remediation, compensation, rollback, and long-tail liabilities.
5. Test rollback and stop mechanisms under realistic permissions, timing, dependencies, and load.
6. Search for prior versions of the recommendation and compare predictions, caveats, and later credit or blame.
7. Record evidence that contradicts the audit and update the ruling explicitly.
