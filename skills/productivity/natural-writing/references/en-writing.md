# English writing and rewriting

Read this reference when drafting, rewriting, or reviewing English. These are contextual editing signals, not proof of AI authorship and not a blacklist.

## Build clear sentences

- Put the subject, action, and object where readers can find them. Prefer active voice when it identifies the actor, but keep passive voice when the receiver or result matters more.
- Use simple verbs such as `is`, `has`, `does`, and `uses` when they are accurate. Do not inflate them into `serves as`, `stands as`, `boasts`, or `represents` without a reason.
- Prefer common transitions such as `and`, `but`, `if`, and `so`. Formal transitions are valid when the genre and logical relationship call for them.
- Vary sentence length by changing information structure, not by randomly adding fragments or punctuation.
- Keep one stable name for the same subject. Change repeated sentence openings through merging, focus, or syntax, not synonym cycling.
- Use ordinary contractions when they fit the voice. Preserve the formality required by legal, academic, technical, support, or brand material.

## Organize by purpose

- Give each paragraph a job: fact, explanation, example, limitation, comparison, decision, or action. Let paragraph length follow the information.
- Use headings and lists for navigation, procedures, parameters, or comparison. Do not turn every thought into a bold label followed by a colon.
- Keep real causal steps and necessary qualifications. Remove transitions that merely announce the next point or restate the previous one.
- Do not force ideas into groups of three or false `from X to Y` ranges. Preserve real lists, sequences, and ranges.
- End with the last useful fact, judgment, or action unless the genre requires a conclusion, summary, sign-off, or call to action.

## Common editing signals

Edit these only when they create inflation, repetition, unsupported logic, or register mismatch:

- Claims that an ordinary event is pivotal, enduring, historic, symbolic, or part of an evolving landscape without supporting evidence.
- Sales language such as `groundbreaking`, `vibrant`, `breathtaking`, `seamless`, or `must-visit` in neutral prose.
- Unnamed authority: `experts argue`, `industry reports show`, `observers say`, or `some critics believe` without an identifiable source.
- Trailing `-ing` clauses that add a vague consequence, significance, or claim not supported by the sentence.
- Repeated `not only ... but`, `not X but Y`, hidden-truth framing, fake objections, and alternatives no reader actually proposed.
- Formulaic openings such as `Let's dive in`, `Here's what you need to know`, or staged candor such as `Honestly?` when they delay the point.
- Stacked qualifiers such as `could potentially possibly`, generic optimism, forced punchlines, and quotation-ready slogans that replace a specific claim.
- Chatbot greetings, praise, offers to continue, knowledge-limit disclaimers, prompt echoes, and discussion of how the draft was generated.
- Excessive em dashes, title case, decorative bold, emoji, or identical paragraph shapes when these are not part of the writer's sample or target style.

## Protect valid usage

- A single transition, em dash, formal word, short sentence, or repeated opening is not a defect. Look for density, function, and surrounding patterns.
- Keep quotations, titles, proper names, legal language, formulas, code, citations, and quoted examples unchanged unless editing them is explicitly authorized.
- Preserve technical uses of words that may be suspicious elsewhere, such as `gating`, and keep necessary hyphens before nouns. Grammar and domain convention outrank pattern matching.
- Keep real objections, alternatives, risk disclosures, FAQ questions, summaries, and deliberate rhetorical repetition.
- Preserve mixed feelings, uncertainty, asides, humor, slang, and uneven rhythm when they belong to the writer. Do not insert them when they do not.
- Match the quotation-mark and punctuation convention of the destination. Curly or straight quotation marks alone say nothing about authorship.

## English review

Read the result aloud and ask:

1. Does every sentence add a claim, qualification, relationship, or useful transition?
2. Did the rewrite preserve every name, number, date, quote, citation, ranking, condition, and degree of certainty?
3. Does the prose sound appropriate for this audience and genre rather than generically casual?
4. Did revision remove a surface phrase while leaving the same mechanical paragraph structure?
5. Are punctuation and sentence variety doing real work rather than manufacturing personality?
